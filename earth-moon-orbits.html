Eclipse season

The band under the top view shows the same scene edge-on, seen from the June-solstice side with the same horizontal scale. Dotted lines link the Earth, Moon and Sun in both views. Earth swings between its March equinox position (left) and its September equinox position (right), passing in front of the Sun around the June solstice and behind it around the December solstice. Only the sunlit sides of the Earth and Moon are bright.

The Moon's path with Earth's orbit straightened

Earth's orbit is straightened into a line: Earth moves right and the Sun is toward the top. The solid line is the path so far and the dotted line is still to come. The purple band in the top view marks this stretch. Drag sideways to move through time.

The Moon passes on the Sun side (top) around new moon and on the far side (bottom) around full moon. At the real speeds its path only waves and never loops. In this straightened view the path bends away from the Sun near new moon, but Earth's real orbit curves toward the Sun, so the Moon's true path always curves toward the Sun. The Sun pulls on the Moon about 2.2 times as hard as Earth does.

About the calculations

Earth's position comes from solving Kepler's equation with JPL mean orbital elements (J2000, Earth–Moon barycentre). The Moon's position uses the main terms of the lunar theory in J. Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms. Equinox and new/full moon times are accurate to within about half an hour; perihelion and aphelion dates can be off by 1–2 days because of the Moon.

Earth's orbit is nearly a circle (eccentricity 0.0167). The Earth–Moon distance is only about 1/390 of the distance to the Sun, so the Moon's distance is enlarged in the figures.
